Truth be told, I'm as much a customer as I am a RevOps professional. I care about CX not just for the people I offer it to, but for myself.

Customer is king. They pay our salaries. Whatever they say goes. The customer is always right.

You've probably heard this preached so often it's muscle memory now. So if it's true, followed and widely accepted, why do we come across so much bad CX?

I'm a sucker for process gaps and for using technology to bridge them. I'm crazy that way. Even when there's no gap, I'll stress test a system to see if anything breaks. You don't go around poking a sleeping dragon, but I do. Crazy, I know. But I just love finding problems to fix. Mind you, I don't invent them. Not that crazy.

So here are a few things I came across recently that bleed CX, and are probably costing someone money.

## 1. The cancellation that never happened

A customer calls their telco to cancel a service. The request is logged with the call centre. Then the rep makes a mistake, gives the wrong information, or just forgets to update the call.

Three months later, the customer is still doing the rounds of the offices, trying every mood and every channel, calls, visits, the lot. The issue still isn't resolved, and the bills keep coming.

The request went into one system and never reached the one that sends the bills. Nobody owned it from "please cancel" to "cancelled".

## 2. The requirement everyone remembers differently

A system integration project. The BRD was signed and sealed. Only part of the client's SOPs were taken into account, probably after a poor discovery process.

The system has been up and running for over eight months, with gaps, of course. The implementation team calls the missing piece a change request. The client says it was communicated at the very start. The SaaS vendor says it was never part of the product and shouldn't have been sold in the first place. And even if it does get built, it goes into the enhancement queue, to be considered and voted on 12 to 18 months from now.

Three parties, one customer, and nobody owning what was promised. Sound familiar? It's the [discovery problem](/writing/your-client-isnt-difficult/) all over again, eight months later.

## 3. The user who was marked absent

A learning platform has a bug that stops one user from seeing the invite or link to a pre-scheduled live session. The user logs in at the agreed time. The link never shows up.

The system marks them absent. Reality was completely different. So who takes ownership of that loss?

## This brings me to the point I'm trying to make

Three industries, one failure. In every story, the customer fell into a gap between two teams or two systems, and nobody owned them across it.

That's why "customer is king" doesn't stop bad CX. Everyone believes it. Nobody is accountable for the space in between.

## What can technology do in CX?

Quite a lot, actually, but only once someone has decided who owns the gap.

In the telco story, a case with an owner and a deadline, connected to billing and closed only when billing confirms, would have saved three months of office visits. In the integration project, tracing every requirement from discovery to build, with one visible change request process, would have settled the argument before it started. And on the learning platform, a simple check of logins against session joins would have flagged the problem before anyone got marked absent.

What technology can't do is make the rep care, settle what was promised if nobody wrote it down, or decide the make-good: the refund, the rebooking, the apology. That part is still on us.

## Why should founders and the C-suite really care?

Because bad CX is a revenue line, not a mood.

[Qualtrics puts it](https://www.qualtrics.com/news/bad-customer-experiences-put-nearly-4-trillion-at-risk-in-global-sales/) at $3.8 trillion of global sales at risk from bad experiences, with 53% of consumers cutting their spending after one. [PwC found](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2018/04/05/new-study-finds-consumers-would-pay-more-for-better-cx-pwc) that 32% of customers would walk away from a brand they love after a single bad experience, and that people would pay up to 16% more for a great one. And it's not getting better. [Forrester's 2025 CX Index](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/cx-index-2025-results), covering over 275,000 customers, found CX quality in the US and Canada dropped for the fourth year running.

The bit I find most telling comes from [Harvard Business Review](https://store.hbr.org/product/stop-trying-to-delight-your-customers/R1007L): how easy you are to deal with predicts loyalty better than how satisfied people are. The telco customer didn't want to be delighted. They wanted to cancel.

On the top line, happy customers stay, spend more and send others your way. On the bottom line, every repeat call, office visit and escalation is a cost you created yourself.

## Who owns CX, sales or CS?

Neither, on their own. And honestly, that's the problem with the question.

Sales owns the promise. Customer success owns the relationship after the sale. Support owns the fix, and product owns what's possible. Everyone owns their moment, and the customer falls through the gaps in between.

So my answer is this. The CEO owns CX, because only the CEO sits above every team that touches the customer. Every team owns its own moment, with a clear standard for what good looks like. And RevOps owns the handoffs: the systems, data and rules that carry the customer from one team to the next without dropping them.

If CX has no owner at the top, it has no owner at all.

## Is CX much more than just vanity?

It is, if you stop measuring it like vanity.

A satisfaction score on a dashboard is easy to admire and hard to act on. The numbers that actually tell you whether CX is working are boring and operational. How many customers have to contact you more than once about the same thing. How long it takes to actually resolve something, not just respond to it. How many complaints, refunds and escalations you handle, and what they cost. How many customers leave, and what happened right before they did.

Those numbers already sit in your CRM and support tools. They're not vanity. They're the price of every gap you haven't closed.

## In conclusion

"Customer is king" is a slogan. CX is a system.

Kings don't spend three months visiting offices to cancel a phone line. They don't wait 18 months for something they were promised at the start. And they certainly don't get marked absent for a session they turned up to.

The gold standard in CX isn't lost. It just never got an owner. Give it one, give the handoffs a system, and maybe, just maybe, the slogan starts meaning something.
