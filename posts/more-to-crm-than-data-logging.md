I recently met a founder who was making a decision on which CRM to invest in. Or so I thought.

I love technology and the wonders it can create, especially in the CX scheme of things. I live and breathe it, so obviously I'm passionate about it.

This gentleman runs an SME, a few million dollars in turnover, in the infrastructure sector. He's doing fairly well regionally and is in huge expansion mode, looking to break into international markets in the coming years.

We got talking, and the first quarter of the meeting was about what I do and what I love about it. Much like every conversation I have with people, in no time this one landed on CRM. Specifically, why would any company in the infrastructure space even consider a CRM when AI can do most of the data storage?

We debated for a bit, until it came to a point where I knew I wasn't winning this one. So I changed the topic.

But I'm going to use this opportunity to put down my thoughts on what a CRM really is, why it should be used, and why it matters in an industry like his.

## So what is a CRM, really?

Most people think a CRM is a place where salespeople are forced to log contacts and deals. That's the software. The idea is bigger.

Customer relationship management is how a company finds, wins, serves and keeps its customers. The CRM is the system that holds all of that together. I think of it as three things: the company's memory (every conversation, promise, quote and contract, in one place, long after the person who made it has left), its rulebook (how a lead becomes a deal, a deal becomes a project, and who does what at each handoff), and its compass (what's in the pipeline, what's at risk, where to put your money next quarter).

Data logging is the least interesting part of all that. And yes, it's the part AI is about to take over. Fair enough.

## Why should you even use one?

Because without it, your company's memory walks out of the door every time someone resigns.

Without a CRM, the customer relationship lives in inboxes, phones, spreadsheets and a few people's heads. Which works, until it doesn't. Your best business development person leaves, and the relationships leave with them. Sales promises something delivery never hears about. You ask what revenue is coming in next quarter and get three different answers. And when you enter a new market, you start from scratch, because everything you learnt in the first one lives in your head.

For a founder planning international expansion, that last one should keep you up at night.

## What can AI do, and what can't it?

He did have a point. A lot of what made CRMs painful is exactly what AI is good at. It can capture calls, emails and meeting notes without anyone typing. It can summarise an account before you walk into a meeting, draft the follow-up, spot the deal that's gone quiet, and answer questions about your pipeline in plain English.

What it can't do on its own is be the one trusted record the whole company agrees on. It can't decide who gets to see which customer, enforce how a deal moves and who signs off, or take accountability when the forecast is wrong. And without somewhere solid to store things, it doesn't remember reliably over months and years.

Don't take my word for it. Salesforce's own AI research team [tested AI agents on CRM tasks](https://www.theregister.com/2025/06/16/salesforce_llm_agents_benchmark/). They managed about 58% of simple, one-step tasks. On tasks with several steps, that dropped to 35%. They weren't great at keeping confidential things confidential either.

So no, not a reason to ignore AI. Just a reason not to hand it your customer relationships with nothing underneath.

## Can a self-built tool on an LLM really replace a CRM?

You can build one, sure. But think about what you're signing up for: a data model for accounts, contacts, deals, projects and contracts. Permissions, so the right people see the right customers. A trail of who changed what and when. Connections to email, calendar, finance and your project tools. Backups, security, and data rules in every country you expand into.

By the time you've built all that, guess what, you've built a CRM. Except now you own every bug, and you depend on the one person who understands how it works. Which, funnily enough, is the exact key-person risk a CRM was meant to remove.

The better question isn't CRM or AI. It's which CRM your AI will work best on.

## Does it give anything other than reports with questionable data?

Honestly? Often it doesn't. And I get the frustration.

But bad CRM data is almost never a software problem. It's a process problem. Data goes bad when nobody owns it, when there are sixty fields and nobody knows which ones matter, and when people update the CRM for the Monday report instead of for their own work.

The teams doing well treat data as part of the job. In Salesforce's [2026 State of Sales survey](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/state-of-sales-report-announcement-2026/) of over 4,000 sales professionals, 79% of high performers said they prioritise data hygiene, against 54% of the underperformers. Yes, Salesforce sells CRM, so take it with a pinch of salt. Still hard to argue with.

The fix is boring, and it works. Fewer fields, clear owners, and let AI do the capturing so people only have to check it.

## CRM in infrastructure

This is where his question got interesting, because industries like his don't need a CRM less. They need it differently.

Infrastructure, construction and engineering are some of the least digitised industries out there. On [McKinsey's digitisation index](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/operations/our-insights/reinventing-construction-through-a-productivity-revolution), construction ranks second to last in the US and last in Europe. Productivity has grown about 1% a year for two decades, against 2.8% for the world economy.

And look at how these businesses sell. A deal can take a year or more, from first conversation to tender to contract, and nobody remembers month two by month twelve unless it's written down. There are owners, consultants, contractors, financiers and regulators, all with a say. Bids get won, lost or walked away from, and the reasons why are the most valuable data a project business has, usually sitting in a folder someone made once. Projects are won with partners. The contract ends, but the relationship (and the next project) shouldn't. And expanding abroad means new partners, rules and buyers all at once.

When one relationship can be worth millions, losing it because it lived in one person's phone is about the most expensive data problem you can have.

## What is the future of it all?

Funnily enough, it's heading in his direction. AI agents will do more of the logging, chasing and summarising. Gartner expects about a third of enterprise software to include AI agents by 2028, up from under 1% in 2024, [as reported by Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/artificial-intelligence/over-40-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-scrapped-by-2027-says-gartner). The same Gartner research also expects over 40% of those AI agent projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027, thanks to rising costs, unclear value or weak risk controls.

My bet? The ones that survive will be working off clean, connected customer data. So the CRM of the future is one you type into less and rely on more.

## Is it worth the hype, or just another overhead?

It's an overhead if you buy it to log data. It's an asset if you use it to run the business: one record of every customer, one way of handing them between teams, and one view of what's coming.

And you don't need to start big. Start with the decisions you need to make, then the few fields that feed them, then pick the tool.

## In conclusion

I didn't win that debate in the room, and on one point he was right. Nobody should be logging data by hand in 2026.

But a CRM was never about logging. It's about a business that remembers its customers, keeps its promises between teams, and knows where it's going. That matters most for a company about to take what works in one market into five more.

AI won't replace that. If anything, it'll finally make it easy.

Do infrastructure and construction companies need a CRM?|Yes, often more than other businesses. Long sales cycles, tenders, many decision makers, consortium partners and expansion into new markets all depend on remembering relationships over years.
