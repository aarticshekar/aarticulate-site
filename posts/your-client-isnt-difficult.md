I constantly meet people in client-facing roles who talk about the irate client.

The demanding one. The one who has no remorse calling late in the night, and sets deadlines nobody can meet, right in the middle of festivals and holidays.

It's hard to be on the front line, pleasing clients like your life depends on it.

You trade time with your family for them.

You look past the resource crunch, the missed milestones, the cost overruns and the sinking morale, all to keep the logo and add one more marquee name to your list.

You even overlook the silence when an invoice goes out.

And after all that, you still have to chase your money, beg even.

It's annoying. So unfair!

It's a given that it's a tough world out there, but for a moment, that's not what I want to talk about.

I want to talk about how we, consultants and professionals, treat clients. Or rather, how clients feel treated.

## Fifty minutes of monologue

I met a client recently for a pitch.

The one hour meeting had an interesting split: 50 minutes of monologue and 10 reserved for dialogue.

She told me she had worked with five different consultants from five different consulting companies.

Each of them promised her something on delivery, and none of them did it.

She had lost time, money and face, given the entire transformation effort was driven by her.

Understandably, it was a difficult meeting. But I walked out feeling bad for her, for how wronged she had been.

She wasn't being difficult. She had been let down five times over.

## What I think has been a miss

As she went on, I kept noticing things that could have gone wrong.

Here's my list of what usually goes wrong with consulting and project-based engagements, and what it looks like when it does:

- **Partial or limited discovery:** a couple of workshops and a template, and everyone calls it done. The real requirements turn up months later as change requests.
- **Incomplete or incorrect process mapping:** the process gets mapped the way the slide says it works, not the way people actually work. So the system is built for a process nobody follows.
- **Not enough understanding of legacy systems:** the old system has quirks only one person knows about. You find them in testing, when they're most expensive.
- **Stakeholders not involved early:** the team that touches the process last hears about it last. They push back at go-live, and they're usually right.
- **Wrong estimates of cost, time or resources:** the number was set to win the deal, not to deliver it. Everyone spends the rest of the project defending it.
- **Legacy systems not checked for integration:** everyone assumes the two systems will talk. They don't, and the integration becomes a project of its own.
- **Compliance and guardrails not thought through:** legal or security read the design in week ten. Then the rework starts.
- **Scope creep underestimated:** small asks pile up with no process to price them. By the end, nobody can say what was in scope in the first place.
- **Little or no communication on status and impact:** the client hears good news until suddenly there's bad news. Trust goes in a single meeting.
- **Time drag:** a three month project becomes nine. People stop turning up to the steering meetings.
- **Too many changes of hands:** every new face starts from scratch. Decisions get reopened because nobody remembers why they were made.
- **Leadership priorities not understood:** the team builds what's easy, not what leadership actually wanted. The project goes live and nobody upstairs cares.
- **Firefighting with no contingency plan:** every problem becomes an emergency. Nobody has time to think about the next one.

Implementation projects don't fail only because of the implementation partner.

There are factors on the client's side too, a list that rarely gets acknowledged:

- **No agreed scope, goal or objective:** the project kicks off on enthusiasm. Six months in, everyone has a different idea of done.
- **A broken as-is process nobody flags:** the new system just automates the mess. It isn't the implementation partner's job to fix it, but someone has to say it.
- **Stakeholders who don't see eye to eye:** two teams want opposite things. The consultant ends up refereeing instead of building.
- **No line between must-haves and nice-to-haves:** everything is urgent. So nothing gets done well.
- **Missing or prehistoric documentation:** nobody can say how things work today. Discovery becomes archaeology.
- **User groups never thought through:** the system works, just not for the people who have to use it. They go back to Excel.

If I had to pick the one that costs the most, it's discovery.

It costs money, time, resources and reputation, the client's and the consultant's.

And no, I'm not being dramatic. PMI found that nearly half (47%) of unsuccessful projects [missed their goals because of poor requirements](https://www.pmi.org/learning/thought-leadership/pulse/core-competency-project-program-success).

Half! And [52% of projects see scope creep](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/scope-creep-rising-11308).

So why do we still quote fixed numbers with no buffer?

## Where it really goes wrong: the middle

I strongly believe there's more to an implementation project than giving X and getting Y.

Sadly, few people see that part, because we're all busy chasing something. The client is chasing completion.

The consultant is chasing closure, and perhaps money.

When a project kicks off, everyone is interested and bringing their best. It's like a game of chess played by amateurs.

The opening is fantastic, and maybe the endgame is great too. It's the middle you mess up.

That's where motivation goes to die, shortcuts live and bad code gets written.

It's home to "let me just get this done and move on".

Your boss is breathing down your neck to reassign you, so you succumb, and by the end you just want out.

Kind of like a long relationship one person wants out of, and the other takes the "I'm the victim, he dumped me" route.

The client, of course, is the one left behind.

And I'm the common friend who can see both sides of the story, not keen to pick one, but who has to move on.

## What happens when this happens

Reputation goes for a toss first. Sure, you can find a scapegoat and blame everyone you want.

You can perhaps even make back the money. But how do you bring back the time?

You could have used those people to do something else, no?

The money is real too.

McKinsey and Oxford [looked at over 5,400 IT projects](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/delivering-large-scale-it-projects-on-time-on-budget-and-on-value): the large ones ran 45% over budget and delivered 56% less value than promised.

And BCG says [70% of digital transformations fall short](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2020/increasing-odds-of-success-in-digital-transformation), so this is hardly rare.

The sponsor pays with her face. She stood in front of her leadership and said this will work.

Next time she trusts nobody, and can you blame her?

Do a few more like this and your talent leaves.

You could call them opportunists, but they have a life and ambitions too.

Why should they pay the price for bad decisions they didn't make?

And the client gets "difficult". Late nights, weekend calls, deadlines over festivals.

Sound familiar? That's the irate client we started with.

Funny how that works, no?

## How to mitigate it

None of this is rocket science, which is probably why it gets skipped.

Do discovery properly, and get paid for it.

Agree scope, goals and must-haves before anyone builds anything, and if the as-is process is broken, say so out loud.

Check the legacy systems and the compliance rules in week one, before you promise anything.

Bring in everyone who touches the process early, including the people who disagree. Better they argue in discovery than at go-live.

Keep the same faces on the project, and hand over in writing when someone moves on.

Estimate in ranges, write the assumptions down, build in a buffer, and agree how change requests work before the first one lands.

And protect the middle.

Talk every week in plain language, and plan a visible win every few weeks so nobody slides into "let me just get this done".

## In conclusion

Coming back to that meeting. Fifty minutes of her talking did feel long, I won't lie.

But looking back, she was handing me the discovery for free. Everything the last five missed, laid out on the table.

So maybe that's where it starts. Flip the split.

Let the client talk for fifty and we speak for ten, and treat clients the way we'd want to be treated if it were our name on the project.

Will the late-night calls and the payment chasing disappear overnight? Probably not.

But a client who feels heard, knows what's going on and never gets surprised doesn't have much reason to be difficult, now do they?
